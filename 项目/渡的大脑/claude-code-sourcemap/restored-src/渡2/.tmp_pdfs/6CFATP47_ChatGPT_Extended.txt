Rather than being an active participant \r\nin the processes that shape its own generative performances, the entity we are calling \r\nDigital Andy is merely the passive recipient of information that was collated on its \r\nbehalf.\r\nDoes this negate the idea that Digital Andy ought to be understood as an EXAI \r\nsystem? The answer is, unfortunately, unclear, for we are (once again) dealing with \r\na rather unusual state-of-affairs, one that seldom arises in the more familiar (human￾centered) cases. As with the redundancy problem, we will try to solicit support from \r\nDigital Andy, asking the model to consider a situation where the subject of cognitive \r\nextension is not the entity who performs the retrieval process:\r\nWhat Digital Andy seems to be suggesting here is that claims regarding the \r\nextended mind could be sustained, even if an information retrieval process were to be \r\ndelegated to some entity that is not the subject of cognitive extension.\r\nIs that the end of the matter? Probably not, for we have no reason to think that \r\nthe relationship between Otto and George (in the above scenario) is the same as that \r\nbetween Digital Andy and the App. If George is responding to a request from Otto, \r\n13\r\n 242 Page 22 of 30\nSynthese (2025) 205:242 \r\nor George is (somehow) monitoring Otto in a way that anticipates his information \r\nneeds, then perhaps we could persist with claims regarding the extended mind. In \r\nthis case, Otto could continue to be understood as the bearer of dispositional beliefs \r\non the grounds that such (dispositional) characterizations yield a predictively- and \r\nexplanatorily-potent (folk psychological) grip over Otto’s actual and counterfactual \r\nbehavior.26 George would then be a constituent of the “extended cognitive network” \r\nthat underwrites these dispositional ascriptions.\r\nThe problem is that the flow of information within the Digital Andy System makes \r\nit difficult to see how Digital Andy (as the putative subject of cognitive/computational \r\nextension) could be credited with the possession of dispositional properties in quite \r\nthe same way as Otto. At the very least, it is hard to see why Digital Andy ought to \r\nbe regarded as the central element of an “extended cognitive network” that includes \r\n(inter alia) the Digital Andy App. Digital Andy, recall, is being invoked by the App, \r\nnot the other way round. Why, then, should the App be regarded as a constituent \r\nof Digital Andy’s extended cognitive/computational routines, as opposed to Digital \r\nAndy being a constituent of the App’s extended cognitive/computational routines? \r\nThere is nothing about the dynamics of the information flow that would preclude this \r\npossibility. Quite the opposite, in fact. All the information processing routines of the \r\nDigital Andy System begin and end with the App. It is the App that accepts the user \r\nquery, the App that retrieves information from the vector database, the App that posts \r\nthe prompt to Digital Andy, and the App that receives the model’s responses prior to \r\npresenting them to the user.\r\nIn view of this, it is at best unclear that Digital Andy ought to be understood as the \r\nsubject of cognitive/computational extension. To be sure, the matter is not entirely \r\ncut and dried,27 but the best examples of EXAI are arguably those that approximate \r\nthe general features of cases discussed in the active externalist literature. A common \r\nfeature of these cases is that extended processes are triggered by a particular indi￾vidual (e.g., Otto), and such processes also exert effects on that individual (e.g., the \r\nretrieval loop influences Otto’s overt behavior).28 This is not the case with Digital \r\nAndy. In the Digital Andy System, the GPT model plays no role in triggering the \r\nretrieval process, nor does the retrieval process directly influence the GPT model. \r\nThese effects are instead mediated by the Digital Andy App.\r\nUnfortunately, then, there are reasons to think that Digital Andy fails to make the \r\ngrade as an EXAI system. This, however, is not quite the end of the story; for Digi￾tal Andy is just one instance of a more general class of RAG-based LLMs, and not \r\nevery member of this class is beset by the agency problem. Consider, for example, \r\nrecent research into what is dubbed active RAG (e.g., Jiang et al., 2023). In active \r\nRAG, the LLM is given a choice of strategies: it can either rely on inner (parametric) \r\nmemory, or it can initiate a call to external (non-parametric) memory. The resultant information \r\nis then factored into the model’s generative processes, just as we have described for \r\nDigital Andy.\r\nNote that the shift to active RAG addresses concerns that lie at the core of both the \r\nredundancy and the agency problem. The redundancy problem is resolved courtesy \r\nof the way the retrieval loop is coordinated with internal memory. That is to say, the \r\nretrieval process is only invoked when the LLM anticipates a shortfall in its capacity \r\nto produce high-quality responses courtesy of internal memory. At the same time, \r\nactive RAG addresses the worry about the retrieval loop not being sufficiently inte￾grated with the workings of the LLM. More specifically, active RAG gives the LLM \r\ngreater control over the retrieval process, enabling the LLM to make repeated calls to \r\nan extra-systemic resource as a way of bolstering its own performance. In this case, \r\nthe capacity to interact with an external resource could be seen to lay the foundation \r\nfor another sort of dispositional ascription, such as a capacity to generate high-quality \r\nresponses. Such ascriptions, it should be clear, are ones that tend to paper over the \r\nlocation-based distinction between internal and external memory.\r\nMoving beyond the realms of information retrieval, recent research has begun to \r\nexplore the more general opportunities that LLMs have to interact with elements of \r\ntheir online ecology. A nice example of this stems from the effort to connect ChatGPT \r\nwith the Wolfram Mathematica system, yielding a hybrid system called Wolfram \r\nGPT.30 The motivation for this particular form of technological symbiosis stems from \r\nthe inherent limitations of LLMs in regard to certain tasks. Consider, for example, the \r\nfollowing error, reported by Floridi and Chiriatti (2020):\r\nGPT-3 works in terms of statistical patterns. So, when prompted with a request \r\nsuch as “solve for x: x + 4 = 10” GPT-3 produces the correct output “6”, but if \r\none adds a few zeros, e.g., “solve for x: x + 40000 = 100000”, the outcome is a \r\ndisappointing “50000”… Confused people who may misuse GPT-3 to do their \r\nmaths would be better off relying on the free app on their mobile phone. (Floridi \r\nand Chiriatti, 2020, p. 688)\r\nAlthough we were not able to reproduce this particular problem using the current \r\nversion of ChatGPT, we do not doubt the more general point that Floridi and Chiri￾atti (2020) are making here, namely, that LLMs exhibit errors when confronted with \r\ntasks that rely on specialized skills and knowledge (in this case, a capacity for math￾ematical reasoning). As Floridi and Chiriatti (2020) suggest, if one wants to solve \r\na mathematical problem, one would probably be better off relying on a tool that is \r\ntailored for this particular purpose (e.g., a mobile app).\r\n29This judgment relies on the token probabilities associated with a model’s initial response (see Kadavath \r\net al., 2022).\r\n30See https://www.wolfram.com/wolfram-plugin-chatgpt/.\r\n13\r\n 242 Page 24 of 30\nSynthese (2025) 205:242 \r\nNote, however, that a mobile app qualifies as a bio-external resource, and the \r\nhuman user is being asked to solve a problem courtesy of their interaction with this \r\nresource. Given this, it seems that the biological counterpart to the LLM’s trans￾former network (i.e., the individual’s brain) is no better placed to solve this particular \r\nproblem than is the LLM. If it is the case that humans are poorly equipped to solve \r\ncertain types of problems using the resources of the bare biological brain, then why \r\nassume that LLMs (or, indeed, any other type of specialized AI system) would be in \r\na much better position? Floridi and Chiriatti (2020) are probably correct to state that \r\nhumans are better off reaching for an external tool to do their maths. But the same \r\nis arguably true of LLMs. To be sure, LLMs are perhaps ill-equipped to solve cer￾tain sorts of problems, such as those involving complex mathematical calculations. \r\nBut is the technologically-denuded human brain in a radically different position? \r\nAnd if the solution to our own biologically-based limitations is to reach for a bio￾external resource, then why not allow LLMs to do the same. In short, why not simply \r\nacknowledge that, for certain types of problems (including those of the mathematical \r\nvariety), LLMs would be better off ‘reaching’ for an external tool, just as we do?\r\nAs it turns out, this notion of LLMs ‘reaching’ for external tools resonates with \r\nmuch of the recent research into LLMs. Of particular interest are what are dubbed \r\naugmented language models (Mialon et al., 2023). These are LLMs with the capac￾ity to interact with other systems and services, such as the aforementioned merger \r\nof ChatGPT with Wolfram Mathematica. Notable examples of such systems include \r\nthe likes of LaMDA (Thoppilan et al., 2022), Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023), and \r\nMind’s Eye (Liu et al., 2022). In addition, recent research into LLMs has led to the \r\nemergence of so-called agentic LLMs, which have the capacity to trigger retrieval \r\noperations and invoke external services, even in the absence of explicit instructions. \r\nIndeed, readers may have noticed that the latest version of ChatGPT features a search \r\ncapability that enables the LLM to search the Web for query-relevant information, \r\neven in the absence of an explicit instruction to do so.31\r\nEach of these efforts strives to augment the capabilities of LLMs, enabling them to \r\ntackle problems that would otherwise lie beyond the reach of their (internal) neural \r\nnets. In addition, the growing interest in augmented and agentic LLMs is moving us \r\ntowards an era in which the aforementioned agency problem does not apply. And \r\nonce the agency-related problem is removed, what remains? Do we expand the scope \r\nof active externalist theorizing to accommodate the possibility of EXAI? Or do we \r\ninsist that active externalism is best served by a selective (perhaps exclusive) focus \r\non biological forms of intelligence? Either way, the issues that emerge as a result of \r\nthis debate are likely to be just as relevant to our attempt to understand the extended \r\nmind as they are our effort to build the next generation of intelligent systems.\r\n31For more on OpenAI’s efforts to support interaction with external, third-party services, see their ‘actions’ \r\nframework: https://platform.openai.com/docs/actions/introduction.\r\n13\r\nPage 25 of 30 242 \nSynthese (2025) 205:242 \r\n6 Conclusion\r\nWe began by considering two ways in which philosophical knowledge might be \r\nincorporated into an LLM. The first approach is exemplified by the effort to build \r\nDigiDan—a digital replica of the philosopher Daniel Dennett (Schwitzgebel et al., \r\n2024). In this case, machine learning techniques are used to tweak the internal param￾eters of an LLM, thereby assimilating knowledge into what is called internal memory.\r\nThe second approach is exemplified by the effort to build Digital Andy—the focus \r\nof the present paper. In this case, we relied on a technique, called RAG, which uses \r\nexternal memory to condition the responses of an LLM. By applying this technique \r\nto the works of Andy Clark, we were able to tailor the responses of an LLM without \r\nthe need for additional machine learning.\r\nAs it happens, the selective focus on Andy Clark (coupled with the commitment \r\nto RAG) reveals a point of philosophical interest concerning the extended status \r\nof AI systems. In particular, the distinction between internal and external memory \r\nestablishes a point of contact with claims regarding the extended mind—the very \r\nissues with which the real-world Andy Clark is concerned. Ultimately, we rejected \r\nthe extended status of Digital Andy on the grounds that the LLM played no role \r\nin triggering the relevant retrieval loop (i.e., the call to external memory). But this \r\n‘shortcoming’ positions us at the very forefront of LLM-related research, revealing \r\nhow multiple efforts are seeking to equip LLMs with the capacity to inter-operate \r\nwith a wider nexus of online tools and services.\r\nThe upshot is a host of issues and concerns that transcend the realms of both phi￾losophy and technology. Can AI systems have extended minds? Is cognitive exten￾sion a route to Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)? What are the mechanisms that \r\nenable an intelligent system to switch between a purely inner routine and one that \r\nreaches out to a wider web of resources? And what is the peculiar role of language \r\nin enabling both ourselves and the machines we build to press maximal cognitive \r\nand computational benefit from our socio-technical surrounds? Such questions are of \r\nindependent philosophical interest, but they are also, crucially, questions that connect \r\nphilosophical debates about the extended mind with the emerging focus of research \r\ninto LLMs. And perhaps this is the best way of understanding Digital Andy—not \r\nas an EXAI system, but as more of a technological artifact that prompts a shift in \r\nindividual and collective attention, helping to expand the scope of active external￾ism and reveal new lines of philosophical inquiry. There are a number of reasons \r\nto welcome this shift. A consideration of EXAI reveals issues that may be difficult \r\nto discern given a selective preoccupation with human-centered forms of cognitive \r\nextension. In addition, we are beginning to see a degree of convergence regarding the \r\nphilosophical effort to understand the extended mind, and the more practical project \r\nof building intelligent systems. A technologically-inflected version of active external￾ism may be the just the thing we need to prompt new lines of inquiry into the theory \r\nand practice of the extended mind.\r\nSupplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.\r\norg/10.1007/s11229-025-05046-y.\r\nAcknowledgements None.\r\n13\r\n 242 Page 26 of 30\nSynthese (2025) 205:242 \r\nAuthor contributions Paul Smart: data preparation; system implementation; paper writing.
