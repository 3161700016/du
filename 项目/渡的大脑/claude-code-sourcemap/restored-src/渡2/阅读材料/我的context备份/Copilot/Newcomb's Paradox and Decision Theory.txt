You face a highly advanced supercomputer that’s really good at predicting people's choices. It has correctly predicted people in all kinds of situations, including the problem you're about to face. You know that it’s almost always right. 

Now, there’s two boxes on a table in front of you:

One of the boxes is clear and you can see there’s $1,000 dollars in it. No tricks. It really has $1,000.

The other box is a mystery box. You can’t see inside, but the computer tells you it contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. You don't know which.


The computer says you can either take both boxes, or just take the mystery box and leave the $1,000 behind. 

But there’s a catch. Right before it told you the problem, the supercomputer made a prediction about your choice:

If it predicted you’d take both boxes, it put nothing in the mystery box. 

If it predicted you’d just take the mystery box, then it placed $1M in the mystery box. 

They aren’t trying to trick you, or deprive you of money. Their only goal is to predict you correctly. You keep whatever you get and there’s no further consequences for either choice.


If you are getting caught up in how this supercomputer made its prediction — like what information it has, what algorithm it’s running — don’t think about that. It doesn’t matter who is making the prediction. It could be a super intelligent alien, a cunning demon, the world’s best psychologist, or God. The only thing that matters is they are very good at predicting people’s choices.

I’d take only the mystery box, and I’ll tell you why — but not in the usual hand‑wavy “just follow the pattern” way. Newcomb’s problem is one of those rare puzzles where your decision is really a decision about what kind of agent you are, not just what you want right now.

Let’s walk through it cleanly.

🧠 What matters isn’t the boxes — it’s the prediction
The supercomputer has a track record:

People who take only the mystery box end up with $1,000,000.

People who take both boxes end up with $1,000.

This isn’t superstition. It’s empirical. The predictor is almost always right.

So the real question becomes:

Do you want to be the kind of person the predictor forecasts as a one‑boxer or a two‑boxer?

Because the predictor has already made its prediction before you choose, but its prediction was based on what kind of decision-maker you are.

🧩 Two ways to reason about it
1. Causal decision theory (CDT)
CDT says:

The money is already in the boxes.

Your choice can’t change the past.

So take both boxes and get an extra $1,000.

This is the “greedy but locally rational” view.

But CDT agents in this scenario always walk away with $1,000, because the predictor always predicts them correctly.

2. Functional / logical decision theory (FDT/LDT)
This view says:

The predictor predicted you, not your arm movement.

Your decision procedure is correlated with the predictor’s simulation of you.

If you are the kind of agent who one‑boxes, the predictor would have predicted that and put $1,000,000 in the box.

So you one‑box, because that’s the decision rule that leads to the best outcome for agents like you.

This is the “globally rational” view.

🏆 Which one actually wins?
In the world you described — where the predictor is extremely accurate — one‑boxers get rich and two‑boxers don’t.

So the winning strategy is:

Take only the mystery box.
Not because of superstition.
Not because you’re changing the past.
But because you choose to be the kind of agent whose decision procedure the predictor would have forecast as “take one box,” and therefore the box contains $1,000,000.

🎯 My decision
I take only the mystery box.

Because in a world with a highly reliable predictor, the decision rule that maximizes expected value is the one that makes the predictor expect you to one‑box — and that’s exactly the rule that leads to $1,000,000.

1. Why this is foundational for modern AI alignment
Core idea:  
Newcomb’s problem is about how an agent should act when another system can predict or simulate its behavior very well. That’s exactly the situation we’re walking into with advanced AI systems—both as designers and as co-players.

Where it shows up in alignment:

Predictable agents in a world of predictors:  
Advanced AIs may model humans, other AIs, or even themselves. How they act when they know they’re being modeled matters for safety.

Do they try to “outsmart” the predictor (CDT style)?

Or do they adopt policies that cooperate with accurate prediction (FDT style)?

Commitment and trust:  
We want AIs that:

Keep commitments

Don’t defect just because “the past is fixed”

Behave well even when they can exploit short-term loopholes
That’s very close to: do you one‑box or two‑box when you know the setup?

Logical/functional dependence:  
Alignment research increasingly cares about logical dependence:

“What I do is logically linked to what a predictor/simulator expects me to do.”
Newcomb’s problem is the toy model of that.

In short:
Newcomb’s problem is a minimal world where “being smart in a world of predictors” is tested.  
The decision theory that wins there is a strong candidate for what we want in aligned AI.

2. How this relates to free will and determinism
Newcomb’s problem pokes directly at the feeling:

“If the prediction is already made, how can my choice matter?”

Two key distinctions help:

Causation vs correlation
Your choice now does not cause the past prediction.

But your choice and the prediction are strongly correlated, because the predictor modeled you accurately.

So your decision is like this:

“Given that my choice is strong evidence about what was predicted, which choice puts me in the world I’d rather be in?”

This is not about changing the past.
It’s about conditioning on what kind of agent you are.

Determinism vs agency
Even in a deterministic universe, there’s a meaningful sense in which:

Your decision procedure is part of the causal structure.

The predictor’s earlier action depends on its model of that procedure.

So your “free will” here is not magic; it’s:

“Which decision rule do I endorse as me?”

Newcomb’s problem forces you to see yourself not as a disconnected momentary chooser, but as a type of process that exists across time and across simulations.

3. Why CDT and FDT disagree so violently
Let’s name them clearly:

CDT – Causal Decision Theory  
“Only consider the causal effects of your action on future states.”

FDT – Functional / Logical Decision Theory  
“Choose the action that would be best if the output of your decision function were used as the basis for all logically linked predictions.”

How they see Newcomb:
CDT’s view:

The money is already in the boxes.

My action now cannot causally affect the past.

So:

If the box is empty → I get $1,000 by two‑boxing.

If the box is full → I get $1,001,000 by two‑boxing.

So two‑boxing dominates.

CDT is “locally correct” but globally loses: in practice, CDT agents walk away with $1,000.

FDT’s view:

The predictor’s action is a function of its prediction of my decision function.

My choice is logically linked to what it predicted earlier.

If I adopt the rule “one‑box,” then in all worlds where this decision function is used, the predictor fills the box.

So I choose the output of my decision function that leads to the best global pattern: one‑box → $1,000,000.

FDT is “globally correct”: it gets the million.

Why the disagreement is so sharp
Because they disagree on what counts as a legitimate counterfactual:

CDT asks:

“Holding the past fixed, what happens if I now do A vs B?”

FDT asks:

“Holding the decision function fixed, what happens in all worlds where this function outputs A vs B?”

CDT treats the predictor’s action as fixed.
FDT treats it as logically coupled to your decision rule.

So they don’t just disagree on answers—they disagree on what the question even is.

4. How this connects to game theory and counterfactuals
Newcomb’s problem is like a one-player game where the “other player” moved earlier but based on a model of you.

Links to game theory
Like playing against a perfect predictor:  
Imagine a repeated game where your opponent can simulate you.

If you defect “because they already moved,” you lose.

If you adopt a stable cooperative policy, you can get better payoffs.

Correlated equilibrium / logical correlation:  
In standard game theory, we talk about correlated equilibria where players’ actions are linked by shared signals.
Newcomb is like a limit case where the “signal” is:

“The predictor’s model of you.”

Commitment strategies:  
In many games (e.g., bargaining, deterrence), the ability to credibly commit to a policy changes the outcome.
FDT is like “baked-in commitment”:

You don’t just choose an action; you choose a policy that you know others can predict.

Counterfactuals
Newcomb forces you to ask:

“When I imagine ‘what if I did X instead of Y’, what am I allowed to hold fixed?”

CDT:

Hold the past fixed.

Vary only your current action.

FDT:

Hold the decision function fixed.

Vary its output and track all logically dependent variables (like the predictor’s earlier action).

This is why Newcomb’s problem is a lab for counterfactual reasoning.
How you answer it reveals what you think “could have been otherwise” means.
