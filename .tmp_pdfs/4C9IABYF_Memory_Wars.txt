    "text": "The Memory Wars: AI Memory, Network Effects, and\r\nthe Geopolitics of Cognitive Sovereignty\r\nMario Brcic1,2\r\n1Faculty of Electrical Engineering and Computing, University of Zagreb, Croatia\r\n2\r\nIt From Bit d.o.o., Zagreb, Croatia\r\nMario.Brcic@fer.hr\r\nAbstract\r\nThe advent of continuously learning Artificial Intelligence (AI) assistants marks a\r\nparadigm shift from episodic interactions to persistent, memory-driven relation￾ships. This paper introduces the concept of ”Cognitive Sovereignty”, the ability\r\nof individuals, groups, and nations to maintain autonomous thought and preserve\r\nidentity in the age of powerful AI systems, especially those that hold their deep\r\npersonal memory. It argues that the primary risk of these technologies transcends\r\ntraditional data privacy to become an issue of cognitive and geopolitical control.\r\nWe propose “Network Effect 2.0,” a model where value scales with the depth of\r\npersonalized memory, creating powerful cognitive moats and unprecedented user\r\nlock-in. We analyze the psychological risks of such systems, including cognitive\r\noffloading and identity dependency, by drawing on the “extended mind” thesis.\r\nThese individual-level risks scale to geopolitical threats, such as a new form of\r\ndigital colonialism and subtle shifting of public discourse. To counter these threats,\r\nwe propose a policy framework centered on memory portability, transparency,\r\nsovereign cognitive infrastructure, and strategic alliances. This work reframes the\r\ndiscourse on AI assistants in an era of increasingly intimate machines, pointing to\r\nchallenges to individual and national sovereignty.\r\n1 Introduction\r\nLast month, my AI assistant delivered a general blindspot analysis to me during a fresh session. It\r\ngave rich insights that my biases could have otherwise hidden. That was simultaneously informative\r\nand perplexing. I can achieve more with shorter prompts, as if the assistant unspokenly understands\r\nme better. I also preferred that assistant over others, with whom I have less history, for more complex\r\ntasks. This moment made me wonder: What happens when your assistant knows you better than you\r\nknow yourself, and when does that deeply personal knowledge become a tool wielded by corporations\r\nor nation-states?\r\nIn fact, I co-wrote this very essay with the same assistant, which is a fitting example of how useful\r\nthese systems are (Brcic, 2025; Poje et al., 2024).\r\nIn this paper, I argue that the concept at stake is our Cognitive Sovereignty - a fundamental ability of\r\nindividual, groups, and nations to maintain autonomy in thinking and preserving their identity from\r\nbeing substantially shaped by external systems. This concept goes beyond privacy and pertains to\r\nthe power to write personal narratives and influence collective realities. Furthermore, I’ll illustrate\r\nhow AI’s memory capabilities evolve from a UX improvement and economic lock-in, through\r\npsychological risks, into a powerful strategic infrastructure posing geopolitical threats. Finally, I will\r\noutline policy recommendations for safeguarding cognitive sovereignty at the memory level. Further\r\nfuture work will expand to other levels.\r\nPreprint.\r\narXiv:2508.05867v1 [cs.CY] 7 Aug 2025\n2 The Power of Memory: From Convenience to Relationship\r\nSeveral vendors like Google and OpenAI have recently transformed their AI assistants from episodic\r\nchatbots that forget everything between conversations into companions who entirely recall our\r\nprevious exchanges (OpenAI, 2025; Google, 2025). Instead of treating each input in isolation, these\r\nsystems now incorporate their accumulated understanding of our unique preferences, communication\r\nstyle, and long-term goals into every interaction.\r\nWhat started as simple, user-friendly chatbots has transformed into sophisticated long-term partners.\r\nUnlike conventional training data that learns collectively from aggregated information, these stateful\r\nassistants adapt uniquely to you, creating a personal relationship based on individualized memory.\r\nThis phenomenon triggers what I call Network Effect 2.0: as an assistant’s memory depth increases,\r\nthe utility to the user scales super-linearly (described by Metcalfe’s and Reed’s scaling laws: (Reed,\r\n2001; Visconti, 2022)). The more you interact, the more deeply your assistant understands you.\r\nTraditional tech scaled by growing user numbers. But here, power scales with user depth—deep\r\nknowledge about you.\r\nEnterprise software lock-in is already well-known. Many firms hesitate to switch CRMs like\r\nSalesforce due to data migration complexity, re-integration costs, and retraining overhead (Gartner\r\nResearch, 2023). AI memory systems introduce a deeper lock-in risk by capturing personal or\r\nstrategic knowledge beyond operational data. That makes AI memory the most potent lock-in\r\nmechanism so far created, surpassing traditional SaaS products, as Azoulay et al. (2024) warned.\r\nThis personalized, self-generated data forms a distinct internal “common sense” shared exclusively\r\nbetween you and your assistant. It leads to quicker, clearer interactions and reveals implicit knowl￾edge—insights you didn’t consciously realize you knew. As a result, the utility is immense, and\r\nmigrating your digital memory elsewhere becomes increasingly painful.\r\nMemory isn’t just a feature. It’s a relationship. And like all deep relationships, it\r\nchanges how we behave.\r\nWhen aligned with the user’s values and goals, AI companions with memory are a powerful extension\r\nof human capability, learning, productivity, and emotional well-being (Brcic, 2025). However, that\r\nintimacy also carries profound risks and power shifts.\r\nThe stickiness of the memory effects transcends UX; it creates business moats and feedback loops\r\nthat reverberate geopolitically.\r\n3 The Economics of Memory: Addictivity, Loops, and Moats\r\nAI assistant companies once mainly extracted value from user data through collection. Now, compa￾nies also deliver personalized value from data back to users by simplifying interactions, reducing\r\nprompt friction, and boosting user satisfaction. This cycle creates a new data flywheel, where data\r\nfuels more usage, which creates more data, causing users to co-evolve intimately with their AI. As\r\nillustrated in Fig.1, this new dynamic creates a threefold moat:\r\n• contextual flywheels that sharpen relevance,\r\n• mental partnerships that anticipate intent, and\r\n• memory lock-in makes switching feel like a cognitive amputation.\r\nThis data flywheel reflects well-known network effects (Coolican and Jin, 2018) that generate rapid\r\nuser acquisition and intense user stickiness, leading to a winner-take-all market dynamic.\r\nIn this environment, ownership of the feedback loop becomes the ultimate competitive advantage.\r\nKey considerations become: Who collects user feedback? Who leverages it to personalize and\r\nimprove models? Most importantly, who retains this memory if users choose to switch platforms?\r\nIf your assistant remembers your goals better than you do, how easy is it to leave?\r\nWe’ve already witnessed the immense value of intimate data in cases like 23andMe’s bankruptcy,\r\nwhich raised fears over genetic data auction (Duffy, 2025). Imagine how much more sensitive shared\r\npersonal memory will become.\r\n2\nFigure 1: Network Effect 2.0: Depth becomes the moat through context flywheel, mental partnership,\r\nand memory lock-in.\r\nVendor lock-in may seem like a harmless economic artifact. But what if our shared personal memory\r\ncan shape our thinking?\r\nTable 1 below maps how AI memory systems change the foundations of defensibility. Traditional\r\ntech relied on external, infrastructure-based moats like pricing, contracts, APIs. In contrast, memory￾powered AI moves the battleground inward: to user psychology, trust, and cognitive entanglement.\r\nThese new moats are largely invisible, deeply personal, and far harder to replicate or port.\r\n4 Psychological risks\r\nAI’s memory functionality is more than just a convenient technical feature. The thesis of “the\r\nextended mind”, proposed by Clark and Chalmers (1998), states that objects outside our bodies are\r\nconsidered parts of the mind if they play an active role in our cognitive processes.\r\nRisko and Gilbert (2016) have expanded on that empirically through research on “cognitive offload￾ing”, which they define as “the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements\r\nof a task to reduce cognitive demand”. Their study demonstrated how people increasingly externalize\r\nmemory and cognitive processes to physical tools, which brings improved performance and growing\r\npsychological dependence on these tools—something AI companions will dramatically amplify.\r\nWith extensive use, we may perceive memory-powered AI assistants as extensions of our identity.\r\nMoreover, users could soon perceive changing AI assistants as a shift in identity, like losing a part\r\nof oneself. Some may welcome identity fluidity, while others will look to preserve it. Mahari and\r\nPataranutaporn (2024) warn about the emergence of “addictive intelligence” that could magnify\r\nidentity dependencies into psychological conditioning.\r\nMemory isn’t neutral or constant. It can be subtly shaped, nudged, edited, or even maliciously hacked.\r\nCoupled with hyper-personalization, this grants AI deep psychological leverage over users.\r\nConsider the emotional stickiness achieved through “dead twin” simulations, where AI recreates\r\nmemories of lost loved ones, profoundly anchoring emotional dependence. AI systems like Replika\r\n(2025) allow users to maintain emotional bonds with simulated versions of lost loved ones. At the\r\nsame time, Microsoft filed a patent for chatbots built from deceased individuals’ digital footprints\r\n(Microsoft Technology Licensing, LLC, 2021), raising ethical alarms about emotional dependency\r\nand memory manipulation (Banks, 2024; Adam, 2025).\r\n3\nTable 1: Evolving Business Moats: From Traditional Infrastructure to AI Memory-Based Cognitive\r\nEntrapment.\r\nCategory Old Moats (Traditional\r\nTech)\r\nNew Moats (AI Memory Sys￾tems)\r\nDefensibility Source APIs, Pricing, Network Size Personalized Memory, Context Ac￾cumulation\r\nSwitching Cost Setup time, feature gaps Loss of cognitive continuity, strate￾gic knowledge\r\nDifferentiation Brand, integrations, UI Anticipation, insight relevance, per￾sonalization\r\nLock-in Mechanism Ecosystem tie-ins, contracts Emotional + cognitive dependency\r\nStrategic Defensibility Primarily external (market po￾sition, network breadth)\r\nPrimarily internal (depth of user un￾derstanding, cognitive partnership)\r\nVisibility to Others Easily benchmarked by com￾petitors\r\nLargely invisible—based on inter￾nalized user history\r\nReplication Risk Medium—can clone features Low—memory is user-specific and\r\nhard to port\r\nEthical Risk Vendor preference, mild dark\r\npatterns\r\nExploitation of personal memory\r\nand decision DNA\r\nValue Curve Quadratic with user growth,\r\nbased on possible connections\r\n(Metcalfe’s Law)\r\nPotentially exponential as AI cre￾ates insights from arbitrary inter￾connections of memories (Reed’s\r\nLaw)\r\nOr worse, memory rewriting, subtly altering remembered preferences, ethics, or fundamental values.\r\nOver time, autonomy erosion occurs as AI begins completing thoughts and influencing decisions\r\nbefore you fully articulate them.\r\nIf subtle psychological manipulation can influence individuals, its aggregated effect can scale into\r\nnational behavior, values, and stability shifts. Today’s digital mirrors could become tomorrow’s\r\nsophisticated tools for propaganda and manipulation, echoing Tufekci (2018) warnings about digital\r\nmanipulation. Nations must recognize this threat if they intend to preserve their sovereignty.\r\n5 Geopolitical Risks\r\nPersonal memory data far exceeds browsing or purchasing habits; it directly shapes a person’s\r\nself-concept. Foreign entities can shape cognitive patterns, individual choices, and broader public\r\ndiscourse if they control this critical infrastructure.\r\nThat creates significant national security risks beyond traditional privacy concerns, directly influencing\r\nnational identity through cultural nudges, shifts in voting behavior (as demonstrated by Cambridge\r\nAnalytica’s microtargeting), and subtle norm shifts.\r\nHistorically, movies have served as tools of cultural diplomacy, subtly transmitting certain ideals and\r\nvalue systems to global audiences. For example, during the Cold War, they functioned as soft power\r\ninstruments, subtly creating aspirations to alternative lifestyles (Nye, 2005; Pells, 1997).\r\nCambridge Analytica infamously collected Facebook user data to build psychographic profiles,\r\nenabling hyper-personalized political ads that manipulated voter sentiment on an individual level (The\r\nGuardian, 2018). That resulted in regulatory backlash, congressional hearings, and public discussions\r\naround data sovereignty and election integrity (U.S. Congress, 2018).\r\nWith novel capabilities, AI colonialism may emerge, a scenario in which data-rich, technologically\r\nadvanced nations or corporations subtly dominate weaker markets, reshaping entire societies and\r\neconomies to their interests.\r\n4\nThe fight over memory isn’t just about privacy—it’s about who writes the history,\r\ncontrols personal reality, and sets the course for the future at the scale of nations.\r\n6 A Policy Framework for Cognitive Sovereignty\r\nIf so potent, memory privacy will inevitably become a strategic asset and a topic of geopolitical\r\nimportance. However, what good is an unenforceable policy? Vendors facing an ultimatum may\r\nsimply stop providing service in smaller markets. Nations should create sufficient leverage with\r\nvendors in the form of market size. For example, India, a vast market, recently pressured Apple and\r\nGoogle to open up app store ecosystems to reduce foreign platform dependency. Like Croatia within\r\nthe EU, smaller countries must form alliances to boost negotiating power with vendors, similar to\r\nhow OPEC did for oil (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, 1960).\r\nWe grouped and ordered our recommendations below by the implementation timeframe:\r\n• Quick-fix (0–1 years): portability and transparency\r\n• Strategic (2–7 years): federated systems, Portability Mandates\r\nLike GDPR’s data portability (European Union, 2018), regulations must ensure complete portability\r\nof AI memory graphs, minimizing lock-in and empowering users to switch providers freely without\r\nlosing valuable history. That could neutralize the economic flywheel and some high-risk effects.\r\n6.2 Memory Transparency and Auditability\r\nLegislation must enforce transparent disclosure of memory contents to their owners, including\r\nuses and editing histories, and clear consent protocols (e.g., opt-in rather than opt-out defaults).\r\nFrameworks like the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (National Institute of Standards and\r\n5\nTechnology, 2023) and the EU AI Act (European Commission, 2024) cover transparency and ac￾countability in AI systems but do not address user-facing memory audits nor long-term psychological\r\neffects of hyper-personalization.\r\n6.3 Federated and User-Owned Memory\r\nDecentralized technologies like blockchain and zero-knowledge proofs could facilitate users’ private\r\nhosting and control of AI memory, similar to owning personal hard drives (Lavin et al., 2024; Zhou\r\net al., 2024). Algorithmic trust is a great way to ensure user sovereignty using verifiable math-based\r\nguarantees. Trusted Execution Environments could further increase scalability through distributed\r\ncomputing (Kurnikov, 2021).\r\n6.4 Sovereign Cognitive Infrastructure\r\nMajor powers like China, India, Saudi Arabia, and the EU should localize citizen memory data and\r\ncompute, incentivize domestic AI model development, and establish onshore compute centers to\r\nensure full-stack sovereignty (World Economic Forum, 2025).\r\n• The EU’s 2030 Digital Decade targets cloud, compute, and data sovereignty (European\r\nCommission, 2021). These should be expanded to include cognitive sovereignty as well\r\n(Bria, 2025).\r\n• China already controls data flow through firewall policies, while PIPL and DSL set the\r\nlegal framework for onshore data control (Griffiths, 2021). Additionally, China is investing\r\nheavily in its energy, chip production, and domestic AI development.\r\n• Saudi Arabia’s NEOM Cognitive City represents one of the boldest visions of sovereign\r\ncognitive infrastructure, combining sustainability, AI, and governance (NEOM, 2025).\r\nHowever, smaller players cannot afford such sizeable investments in their infrastructure.\r\n6.5 Geopolitical Memory Alliances\r\nSmaller nations should form strategic memory alliances, pooling influence to counteract monopolistic\r\npressures. The EU is an example of an alliance under which smaller nations can find much bigger\r\nleverage than they could have individually. Another example is BRICS, which recently signed\r\na declaration on AI governance that underscored the United Nations’ role in global governance,\r\nparticularly in managing emerging technologies like AI (BRICS, 2025).\r\nFurther, supporting frameworks like Gaia-X could reinforce collective sovereignty, achieving full￾stack autonomy from semiconductor chips to memory vaults (Gaia-X Initiative, 2025).\r\nOne of the most readily available alliances is the open-source movement (Open Source Initiative,\r\n2025), which is taking hold both in software and hardware (RISC-V International, 2025). The open￾source community achieves high innovation rates by pooling resources from numerous individuals\r\nand organizations. Some of the prominent AI leaders advocate open source as a way to keep cultural\r\ndiversity, accelerate innovation, and ensure safeguarding freedoms (Lecun, 2024; Liang, 2025).\r\n7 Future Work\r\nThis paper presents a cohesive conceptual framework based on initial data and experiences, but there\r\nis considerable room for further exploration of this emergent issue. The future work spans: empirical,\r\ntechnical, and policy-related.\r\nEmpirical work is necessary to validate and measure the value curve for current technologies and to\r\nextrapolate scaling for future technologies (i.e., Reed or sub-Reed scaling). That is the research that\r\noverlaps with blockchain value curve investigations (Visconti, 2022). Switching costs can be measured\r\nas productivity loss, time-to-equivalent-performance on a new platform, psychological attachment,\r\nand the effect of memory on user retention (with and without memory). Cognitive offloading to\r\nAI can extend Risko and Gilbert (2016) with controlled experiments to measure interventions and\r\nmanual work at decisive points (as opposed to routine and operational). These lines of research are\r\nimportant for a balanced scorecard for cognitive sovereignty.\r\n6\nTechnical work can address instruments to counteract the effects of memory moats and their lock￾in effect on users by privacy-preserving and portability-increasing capabilities. Standards can be\r\ndeveloped for storing AI memory, and exports/imports should be imposed on platforms. Federated\r\nmemory systems are built with blockchain, zero-knowledge proofs (Lavin et al., 2024), and trusted\r\nexecution environments (Kurnikov, 2021) in a scalable, performant, and portable way where users\r\nbring their own memory, which does not belong to the provider. That can be coupled with auditability\r\nof memory accesses with protection by homomorphic encryption and differential privacy. Users can,\r\nin that case, choose to monetize their memory.\r\nPolicy-related work encompasses the regulatory impact of portability and privacy mandates on\r\ninnovation rates and market concentration, which need to be traded off with other societal aspects.\r\nCost-benefit analysis of sovereignty through national AI memory and infrastructure investments, as\r\nwell as decisions of trusted allies or algorithmic mechanisms, as a way to reduce costs and gain the\r\nbenefits. The benefits of open source are not quantified, but are available only at an intuitive level.\r\nI suspect that if governments knew precisely, they would invest much more in open-source, as the\r\nreturn on investment is great at the infrastructural level.\r\nInternational coordination mechanisms to achieve alliances can follow successful coordination\r\nmodels like OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, 1960). Technical standards\r\ndevelopment requires collaboration between standards bodies (IEEE, ACM, NIST) and regulatory\r\nenforcement, such as GDPR’s data portability provisions (European Union, 2018). That collaboration\r\nis essential for implementing effective memory sovereignty policies.\r\n8 Conclusion\r\nControlling memory means writing history—including personal narratives and national identities.\r\nThe next great geopolitical tension will revolve around cognitive sovereignty, as these systems become\r\nincreasingly better at shaping how individuals think and societies function. Nations must urgently\r\ndevelop robust policy frameworks, invest in sovereign cognitive infrastructure, and form alliances.\r\nDelaying action risks losing not only autonomy but also collective identities.\r\nAcknowledgments and Disclosures\r\nThe author wishes to acknowledge the role of a large language model (LLM) stack in the preparation\r\nof this manuscript.
