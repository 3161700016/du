ACM, New York, NY, USA, 16 pages.\r\nhttps://doi.org/10.1145/nnnnnnn.nnnnnnn\r\n1 INTRODUCTION\r\nIn spite of the giant leap-forward of AI technologies in the past\r\ndecade, the human cognitive ability still far eludes machine systems.\r\nThe situation will likely remain for long, since the current wave\r\nof AI resolution heavily relies on massive data, which differs from\r\nhuman cognition in principle, and is unlikely to surpass human’s\r\nability of generalization and logical reasoning. To fundamentally\r\novercome this limitation, AI can augment or even integrate with\r\nhumans, instead of a replacement [21]. One way to attain this vision\r\nis to develop intelligent feedback to improve human cognition per￾formance. For example, by properly visualizing brain wave patterns\r\nto human users as a neurofeedback, the users could self-regulate\r\ntheir emotion status [26]. Such neurofeedback needs to be judi￾ciously presented, as a constant feedback may increase anxiety and\r\narXiv:2508.00846v1 [cs.HC] 9 Jul 2025\nConference ’23, 2023, Location Xu, et al.\r\nreduce cognitive performance. Recent research (e.g., AttentivU[32]\r\nand Dan et al. [66]) proposed to adapt the feedback over time to\r\nimprove user engagement. When the system detects a drop of user\r\nengagement, it triggers corresponding feedback to regulate users\r\nimmediately. However, this is a myopic feedback control strategy,\r\nand may not improve the overall performance across a cognitive\r\nprocess. Therefore, intelligent feedback to regulate user perfor￾mance is a general problem that still needs further research.\r\nIn this paper, we seek to answer a general question in realizing\r\nhuman-AI integrated systems, i.e. can we leverage AI models to aug￾ment and improve human cognition performance, through intelligent\r\nand continuous feedback? We explore the answers through a rep￾resentative cognitive task, i.e., math arithmetic problem solving\r\n(Sec. 3), which is widely used in evaluation of human cognition\r\nbehaviors[20, 22, 31, 35]. We use time pressure as the feedback\r\nmodality. Time pressure, presented through a visualization of time\r\npassing, has been proven to be effective in regulating cognition\r\nperformance[15, 41, 61]. It can increase the arousal of users[24] by\r\ndelivering a sense of urgency. However, a too strong time pressure\r\nmay distract and stress users, thus degrading their performance.\r\nExisting research in cognitive science has demonstrated this trade￾off [61, 74], but to our knowledge, no systematic solutions have\r\nbeen proposed and verified to adapt time pressure in real-time to\r\ncontinuously regulate human cognition. Ideally, an adaptive algo￾rithm to control the existence of time pressure feedback according\r\nto users’ real-time performance can attain a sweet-spot. However,\r\nit is challenging to design such adaptation, as human cognition\r\nbehaviors are highly dynamic and, unlike machine systems, do not\r\nfollow closed-form models.\r\nDeep reinforcement learning (DRL) may automate such closed￾loop adaptation. At a high level, improvement of user performance\r\nwill result in larger reward for a DRL agent, which encourages it to\r\nlearn the policies of adaptive time pressure that maximizes future\r\nreward, thus improving overall user performance throughout an\r\nentire cognitive task. However, DRL needs to be substantiated with\r\na massive amount of training data. Even for well-defined math\r\narithmetic tasks, it is infeasible to allow the DRL agent to interact\r\nwith real users online to update its model, especially considering\r\nthe slow paces of human cognitive tasks. In addition, whenever\r\nthe hyperparameters change, the DRL model has to be retrained\r\nthrough a new user study. As a result, the iterative exploration of\r\nhyperparameters, which is taken for granted in machine perception\r\ntasks, becomes infeasible in such AI-mediated human cognitive\r\ntasks. Moreover, the initial exploration of the DRL agent training is\r\nusually random, which may jeopardize user performance.\r\nTo tackle these problems, we propose a dual-DRL agent. Specifi￾cally, a regulation DRL agent (Fig. 1) controls the existence of time\r\npressure feedback, while interacting with a simulation DRL agent\r\nthat mimics real user cognition behaviors. Specifically, in the math\r\narithmetic task, given specific math question and time pressure\r\nfeedback as input, the simulation DRL agent could output corre￾sponding user cognition performance, including answering accu￾racy and response time. The simulation DRL agent is pre-trained\r\n(Fig. 1(1)) based on an existing dataset in our prior work [71]. It\r\nserves as virtual users to train the regulation DRL agent (Fig. 1(2)),\r\nthus overcoming the aforementioned human data crunch problem.\r\nFor each math question, the regulation DRL agent decides whether\r\ntime pressure should be delivered to virtual users according to\r\ntheir past performance. It will receive more reward if virtual users’\r\nperformance gets improved. This reward design encourages the\r\nregulation DRL agent to learn potential strategies to optimize vir￾tual users’ performance. Upon convergence, the trained model can\r\nbe used to regulate cognitive performance of real users (Fig. 1(3)).\r\nWith the dual-DRL agent design, the exploration capability of\r\nthe DRL model can be massively enhanced as the interaction steps\r\ncan be unlimited and hyperparameters can be easily iterated in the\r\nsimulation environment. In addition, the random exploration of\r\nregulation DRL agent in the initial process no longer affects the\r\nreal users’ performance.\r\nWe have conducted an N=80 user study to evaluate the perfor￾mance of the dual-DRL agent design. Specifically, participants in\r\nRL group received adaptive time pressure feedback controlled by\r\nregulation DRL agent in the cognition task, whose results were\r\ncompared with another baseline group named Random group. The\r\nfinal results demonstrate that the participants in RL group could\r\nachieve better performance than the baseline group. Moreover, our\r\nfurther analysis of individual performance change and the time\r\npressure trajectory demonstrate the effectiveness of the regula￾tion DRL agent, and explain how the regulation DRL agent could\r\naugment and improve user cognition performance. We have pub￾lished the software toolset and dataset1\r\nto encourage replication of\r\nthis study and development of more powerful models to augment\r\nhuman cognition.\r\nTo summarize, the main contributions in this paper include:\r\n• An AI-mediated interaction mechanism to augment human\r\ncognition performance through continuous, adaptive visual\r\nfeedback.\r\n• A dual-DRL framework to address the data inadequacy issue\r\nin the human-in-the-loop training process. The framework\r\ncould be further extended to explore various intelligent inter￾vention/feedback to augment other human cognition tasks.\r\n• A user study of 80 participants to demonstrate the feasibility\r\nand effectiveness of our AI-mediated cognition augmenta￾tion framework.\r\n2 RELATED WORK\r\nOur work draws inspirations from and advances the knowledge in\r\nthe following 4 categories of research.\r\n2.1 Cognition Regulation\r\nCognition performance represents observable behavior in cogni￾tive tasks including logistic reasoning, decision making, problem\r\nsolving, and memorization [20, 37]. One of the common ways that\r\nusers improve cognitive performance in their daily lives is the con￾sumption of beverages such as coffee[11] and energy drinks[16],\r\nas well as psychoactive drugs[42] to increase arousal[20]. How￾ever, the drinks and drugs may lead to health side effects if used\r\nexcessively[13, 20]. Exercise and training[64] could be a safer al￾ternative. Moreover, various real-time intervention systems have\r\nbeen developed to provide real-time feedback that regulates and\r\nimproves user performance. For instance, AttentivU[33] measures\r\n1https://github.com/songlinxu/TimeCare\nCognitive Exoskeleton: Augmenting Human Cognition with an AI-Mediated Intelligent Visual Feedback Conference ’23, 2023, Location\r\nusers’ attention by EEG and provides feedback to increase atten￾tion, which in turn leads to better cognition performance. Recent\r\nadvances in brain-computer interfaces also usher in innovations\r\nin neurofeedback, which present brain wave patterns in a proper\r\nformat to help the users self-regulate their performance. For in￾stance, online auditory neurofeedback could regulate arousal and\r\nattention in a demanding sensory-motor task[26]. Memory could\r\nalso be augmented with real-time intervention systems such as\r\nMemory Glasses[18] and SenseCam[59]. Emotion is another impor￾tant factor in cognition performance. MoodWings[36] could mirror\r\nusers’ stress status in real-time on a wearable biofeedback device\r\nto help users calm down during stressful tasks. EmotionCheck[19]\r\nand BoostMeUp[20] leveraged heart rate-like haptic feedback to\r\nregulate emotion and cognition.\r\nSuch real-time intervention systems either present users’ current\r\nemotion status to facilitate self-regulation, or provide adaptive feed￾back according to users’ performance. The adaptive strategies are\r\nbased on simple first-order heuristics. For example, AttentivU[32]\r\ntriggers vibration feedback when the EEG sensor detects a decline\r\nin user engagement. Such myopic strategies may not always in￾crease user engagement throughout a cognitive process, and may\r\ninstead act as a distraction. Our work presents a closed-loop in￾telligent agent that could control the visual feedback adaptively\r\naccording to the real-time user performance. It could learn the\r\npotential strategies to optimize the feedback over a longer horizon.\r\n2.2 Reinforcement Learning for Human\r\nBehavior Regulation\r\nReinforcement learning (RL) can regulate human behaviors in an\r\ninteractive manner. It has been applied in diverse scenarios such\r\nas education, health, and human-machine interaction. Specifically,\r\nRL could help pacing the educational activities in the online course\r\naccording to real-time learning outcomes of students, which could\r\nincrease learning gains [6]. Liao et al. [34] developed an RL algo￾rithm which continuously learned to improve the treatment policy\r\nembedded in the just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) as con￾text data such as step count variation collected from the user. The\r\nalgorithm delivered adaptive intervention (e.g., context-tailored ac￾tivity suggestion) on mobile devices to help users maintain healthy\r\nbehaviors. In addition, an RL agent could enable a creative work￾flow centred on human–machine interaction, such as exploring\r\nparameter spaces in partnership with users for sound design[57].\r\nThese systems verify the feasibility of leveraging RL to regulate\r\nhuman behaviors. However, RL training requires a large amount of\r\ndata, which is challenging to obtain in human-in-the-loop training.\r\nTo overcome this issue, existing work either opts for a large-scale\r\nstudy involving thousands of participants [6] or a highly simplified,\r\nless data-hungry algorithm [34, 57]. However, large-scale data col￾lection is a tedious task and does not bode well with iterative model\r\ndevelopment, whereas a simplified RL algorithm may lose all the\r\npower from the deep learning wave. Even if recent human-in-the￾loop RL algorithms (such as Deep COACH[3] and Deep Tamer[69])\r\nare more data-efficient than traditional RL algorithms, the hyperpa￾rameter tuning is still an iterative process that requires repetitive\r\nuser studies.\r\nOur framework resolves this dilemma through a dual-DRL model,\r\nwhich first trains a simulation DRL agent to enable the generation\r\nof unlimited data, which is in turn used to train the regulation DRL\r\nagent for user intervention. This also enables hyperparamter tuning\r\nwithout tedious iterations of user studies.\r\n2.3 Cognition Models\r\nExisting research in cognitive science has explored a variety of\r\ntheoretical models to simulate or predict cognition performance.\r\nExample cognitive models include human-display interaction[14]\r\nand computational models such as BEAST[25] and Drift-Diffusion\r\nModel[55, 65]. Chen et al. proposed a model to capture human\r\nperformance in the credit card transaction task[14], by learning\r\nhow eye movement patterns vary in response to data presenta￾tions. Steyvers et al. introduced [65] a probabilistic computational\r\nmodel to describe different user task-switching behaviors across the\r\nlifespan[65]. In addition to building specific models for cognition\r\ntasks such as Point-and-Click task[23, 47], RL has been used to\r\nspeed up inference with user simulators in point-and-click tasks\r\n[40]. Inspired by the powerful feature representation and function\r\napproximation capabilities of neural networks, deep learning-based\r\ncognitive models have been developed to mimic human cognition\r\nbehaviors such as decision making[10, 45, 51, 52] and categoriza￾tion [7–9, 50, 60]. Notably, recurrent neural network (RNN) could\r\nsimulate the tradeoff between user response time and accuracy in\r\nbiological vision [63]. In addition, by integrating cognitive model\r\npriors, deep learning models could simulate human decision making\r\nbehaviors more accurately[10].\r\nCompared with these existing models, the cognition model in\r\nour framework leverages the recent advances of deep reinforcement\r\nlearning and integrates the cognition prior model (Drift-Diffusion\r\nModel) to simulate user cognition behaviors in a more fine-grained\r\nmanner. Specifically, our simulation model can mimic the progres￾sive effect of external stimuli, instead of merely predicting the result\r\nof each cognition task.\r\n2.4 Time Pressure Feedback\r\nExisting work has pointed out the effectiveness of time pressure\r\nin regulating human cognition [15, 41, 61], decision making[24],\r\nworking efficiency [5], etc. For instance, by controlling ticking rate\r\nof a virtual clock, users’ work productivity could be improved\r\nunconsciously[5]. Although such time pressure could improve cog￾nitive performance by increasing focus[70] and arousal[24], it may\r\nalso lead to high anxiety, resulting in worse performance. In ad￾dition, different levels of time pressure may have different effects\r\non user behaviors. For example, for risky choice behaviors, users\r\nunder high time pressure tend to spend more time focusing on the\r\nnegative parts, but those under low time pressure prefer to observe\r\nthe positive facts[74]. Higher time pressure may also lead to ex￾cessive mental workload and deteriorated response [15]. Moreover,\r\nhigh time pressure may increase cognition errors, as revealed in a\r\nvisuomotor task study [61].\r\nTherefore, time pressure feedback is a mixed blessing for human\r\ncognition. We use accuracy and response\r\ntime as the cognitive performance metrics.\r\n4 TIME PRESSURE DESIGN\r\nWe adopt time pressure as the feedback modality in our dual-DRL\r\nframework, as its effectiveness in regulating user cognition has\r\nbeen well established, particularly for tasks such as attention [15,\r\n61], decision making[24] and productivity[5]. Time pressure could\r\nbe delivered in different forms, e.g., haptic, auditory, and visual\r\nfeedback. Here we adopt visual feedback as it can be delivered to\r\nusers more quickly and efficiently. To convey a sense of urgency\r\nwithout aggravating the cognitive workload, and in particular to\r\nkeep users from focusing too much on the exact time, we present to\r\nusers a simple progress bar instead of a clock as the visual feedback,\r\nalong with the math problem. The progress bar adds one unit per\r\nsecond (Fig. 3(a)). It resets after 5 seconds and starts increment\r\nagain afterwards. In this way, users will only receive a sense of\r\nurgency due to time passage, but will not focus on the exact time.\r\n5 DUAL-DRL AGENT FRAMEWORK\r\nThe overarching goal of our framework is to train a regulation DRL\r\nagent that controls the existence of time pressure feedback during\r\neach trial, i.e., each process where a math question is presented and\r\nthen answered by the user. This regulation DRL agent is first trained\r\nin a simulation environment, which mimics cognition behaviors of\r\nreal users. Then the trained regulation DRL agent is directly applied\r\nto regulate real users in real-time.\r\nIn our framework, the simulation environment is actually an￾other DRL agent (i.e., the simulation DRL agent), which learns to\r\nmimic real user behaviors based on an existing dataset. Given a\r\nspecific math question, trial number and feedback pattern as input,\r\nthe simulation DRL agent will output the corresponding answer and\r\nresponse time, just like what real users will do in the cognition task. In\r\nthis way, the regulation DRL agent could always interact with the\r\nsimulation DRL agent to explore potential time pressure control\r\nstrategies to regulate user cognition performance. The online train￾ing data is unlimited to ensure the convergence of regulation DRL\r\nagent. We could also fine-tune the hyperparameters of regulation\r\nDRL agent and apply them into the new round of interaction with\r\nthe simulation DRL agent. In addition, the random exploration of\r\nregulation DRL agent in the initial training stage will only happen\r\nin the simulation environment instead of real users. Upon con￾vergence, the trained regulation DRL agent could be applied into\r\nreal-world scenarios. We thus only briefly summarize\r\nthe design to avoid redundancy. Before modeling the effect of time\r\npressure, we first need to model users’ basic cognition performance\r\nwithout time pressure, which is achieved by an LSTM (Long Short￾Term Memory)-based math answer agent and an SVM (Support\r\nVector Machine) model (Fig. 2). The math answer agent is trained to\r\nanswer math questions correctly instead of predicting user perfor￾mance. By doing so, the math answer agent could extract potential\r\nfeatures, in the form of intermediate output (Fig. 2), which reflect\r\nuser performance change across different math questions. These\r\nfeatures are then fed into an SVM model to predict a user’s basic\r\nperformance (both accuracy and response time).\r\nThen, in order to simulate the effect of time pressure on user\r\nperformance in a fine-grained manner, we segment cognition pro￾cess into steps. For each step, the corresponding image frame of\r\ntime pressure feedback as well as encoded math questions are fed\r\ninto a DRL loop to simulate the effect of time pressure on user\r\nperformance. The final output is a prediction of user response time.\r\nNote that one frame represents the corresponding time pressure\r\nvisual image during one step. Each math trial is segmented into\r\nsteps with frequency 𝑓 = 5 Hz. For instance, if user response time\r\nfor one trial is 5 seconds, then the math trial comprises 25 steps. The\r\nselection of different frequency values has been discussed in [71].\r\nSuch segmentation affords a more fine-grained model to simulate\r\nthe effect of time pressure.\r\nNote that here the DRL agent only simulates user response time\r\nunder time pressure; whereas the response accuracy is predicted\r\ndirectly from the SVM models. The reason is that users’ speed￾accuracy performance is usually biased by the instruction [47, 73].\r\nIn our experiments, the participants are asked to take accuracy as\r\npriority and then answer questions as soon as possible. As a result, user\r\naccuracy will not be affected by time pressure significantly. Therefore,\r\nwe only use the DRL agent to simulate the effect of time pressure\r\non users’ response time instead of accuracy.\r\n5.1.1 Math Answer Agent. The math answer agent is a sequence￾to-sequence model based on LSTM, which is trained to predict math\r\nquestion answers. We first use sequence encoding to encode each\r\nmath question string into a sequence vector. The sequence vector\r\nis then fed into LSTM layer (256 neurons in the hidden unit), which\r\nis then connected with 17 neurons with the softmax activation\r\nfunction. Finally, the neuron with the highest probability is the final\r\noutput answer. We use Keras[17] to implement the math answer\r\nagent model (loss function: categorical cross entropy, optimizer:\r\nAdam, learning rate: 0.001).\r\n5.1.2 SVM Model: Predicting Baseline Performance. The SVM mod￾els take the features captured by the math answer agent and trial\r\nnumber as input, and predict user’s choice and response time with￾out time pressure. We use an SVM classifier (SVC) and regressor\r\n(SVR) to predict user choice 𝑅𝑐 and response time 𝑅𝑡\r\n, respectively.\r\nThe SVM models are implemented with scikit-learn[49], where we\r\nuse default regularization parameter, kernel, and other parameters\r\nfor both SVC and SVR. The SVC not only predicts user choice but\r\nalso outputs the probability 𝑅𝑝 for each possible choice.\r\n5.1.3 Drift-Diffusion Model and DRL Agent. Our simulation DRL\r\nagent relies on a widely-used cognition model, i.e. Drift-Diffusion\r\nModel (DDM) [27] to mimic user cognition performance. DDM\r\nassumes that users make selection by accumulating evidence for\r\neach choice and the final selection is made when the evidence\r\naccumulator passes a boundary threshold [27]. Here we incorporate\r\nthe SVM model prediction results into DDM. Specifically, we use the\r\noutput probability of SVC as the accumulated evidence, whose start\r\npoint is 0.5. The boundary threshold is 𝑅𝑝 , which is the probability\r\nwhen SVC makes the predictions. When there is no time pressure,\r\nthe evidence accumulator will increase from start point to boundary\r\nthreshold ideally. However, when time pressure is delivered to users,\r\nthe trajectory of evidence accumulator will change and the evidence\r\naccumulator may achieve the boundary threshold earlier or later\r\nthan before, resulting in the new response time of users.\r\nWe incorporate a DRL agent to simulate this effect of time pres￾sure on evidence accumulation process. Specifically, we segment\r\nthe initial evidence accumulation process into steps under specific\r\nfrequency 𝑓 = 5 Hz. In each step, we leverage the DRL agent to\r\nsimulate the effect of corresponding frame of time pressure visual\r\nfeedback on evidence accumulation process. For each step 𝑡 in the evidence accumulation\r\nprocess, the action (𝑎𝑡\r\n) of the simulation DRL agent is a continuous\r\nnumeric value normalized from −1 to 1, i.e. 𝑎𝑡 ∈ [−1, 1]. 𝑎𝑡 = 0\r\nmeans that current time pressure frame has no effect on evidence\r\naccumulator in DDM. 𝑎𝑡 ∈ [−1, 0) means the current time pressure\r\nframe introduces negative change on the evidence accumulator,\r\nand positive otherwise.\r\n5.1.5 Observation Space. The observation space (State: 𝑆𝑡 = (𝑚𝑡\r\n, 𝑝𝑡))\r\nof the simulation DRL agent is composed of both math question\r\ninformation and dynamic time pressure visual stimuli. Similar to\r\nthe math answer agent, for each math trial, the math question in￾formation is encoded as a sequence vector (𝑚𝑡\r\n) in the observation\r\nspace. In accordance to the segmentation of evidence accumulation\r\nprocess, the dynamic time pressure visual stimuli is also segmented\r\ninto frames just like what users will receive in the study. Given\r\nframe rate 𝑓 , for each frame 𝑡, we could obtain the specific image\r\n𝑝𝑡 of time pressure visual stimuli for input in the observation space,\r\nwhich will be extracted into specific features from the default CNN\r\nfeature extractor in Stable Baselines3 [54].\r\n5.1.6 Terminal State. The terminal state is achieved if the evidence\r\naccumulator achieves boundary threshold (𝑅𝑝 ) or the DRL agent\r\nachieves maximum steps in one episode. Here each episode is com￾posed of several steps. When the DRL agent achieves the terminal\r\nstate, one episode ends and a new episode will start. In this sim￾ulation DRL agent, one episode is the same as one math trial. We\r\nset the maximum response time 𝑅𝑇𝑚𝑎𝑥 to be 10 seconds. So the\r\nmaximum step number 𝑆𝑁 = 𝑅𝑇𝑚𝑎𝑥 × 𝑓 = 10 × 5 = 50 steps. If the\r\nevidence accumulator achieves boundary threshold 𝑅𝑝 when DRL\r\nagent takes 𝑆𝑛 steps (𝑆𝑛 < 𝑆𝑁 ), then the new predicted response\r\ntime is 𝑅𝑟𝑙 = 𝑆𝑛/𝑓 .\r\n5.1.7 Reward Function Design. For each step during an episode,\r\nthe simulation DRL agent only gets reward in the terminal state.\r\nThe reward is 0 for other situations. In addition, the working efficiency of people with\r\ndisabilities could be improved by applying the universal design\r\nconcept for working environment enhancement [53]. In addition, virtual communities and mo￾bile platforms could foster student engagement to achieve better\r\nlearning outcomes [46]. Furthermore, ubiquitous sensing in the\r\nclassroom [2] such as EduSense[1] and n-Gage[28] could provide\r\nmore insightful information to instructors and therefore improve\r\nlearning outcome. Similar with our framework to augment user\r\ncognition, existing work has also demonstrated the feasibility of\r\nleveraging reinforcement learning models to adaptively schedule\r\neducational activities and improve student learning performance\r\n[6]. Based on these existing exploration, we believe that it is feasi￾ble and promising to utilize a similar AI-mediated framework to\r\naugment learning outcome via adaptive intervention control.\r\n7.3 Adaptive Mental State Regulation\r\nExisting biofeedback systems usually integrate sensing and feed￾back modalities to regulate user mental states. For instance, Mood￾Wings [36] could allow users to self-regulate their stress status\r\nvia mirroring users’ real-time stress state through actuated wing\r\nmotion. In addition, EmotionCheck leverages bodily signals and\r\nfalse heart-beat feedback to regulate user emotion [19]. By con￾trast, BoostMeUp[20] generates personalized haptic feedback in a\r\ndifferent heart rate to regulate user emotion and improve cognitive\r\nperformance. False heart-rate feedback is also verified to be able\r\nto create an interoceptive illusion of effort on users[30]. Moreover,\r\nonline neurofeedback could also regulate user arousal and therefore\r\nimprove human performance[26]. Finally, biofeedback systems like\r\nAttentivU [33] could integrate EEG to provide adaptive interven￾tion to regulate user emotion. Such real-time intervention systems\r\neither simply present users’ current mental states to facilitate self￾regulation, or provide adaptive but simple first-order heuristics\r\nfeedback to regulate users’ mental states. These myopic strategies\r\nmay not always improve user engagement but may act as a distrac￾tion. Based on our framework, AI-mediated intelligent strategies\r\ncould further improve the adaptation of biofeedback systems to\r\nregulate user mental states in a far-sighted manner.\nConference ’23, 2023, Location Xu, et al.\r\n8 DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS, AND FUTURE\r\nWORK\r\n8.1 Opportunities and Challenges\r\nIn this work, we leverage the pre-trained regulation DRL agent in\r\nsimulation environment to regulate cognition performance of real\r\nusers without tailoring the model to specific users. This framework\r\ndesign solves three main problems of straightforward online DRL\r\ntraining in real user studies. First, it is hard to obtain adequate data\r\nfor DRL agent training in human-involved model training process.\r\nSecond, fine-tuning hyperparameters of the DRL agent may require\r\niterative user studies. Third, the initial exploration of DRL agent in\r\ntraining process may be quite random and user performance may\r\nget very bad if this initial training process directly happens in real\r\nusers. Existing work has also shown the effectiveness of leveraging\r\nreinforcement learning to regulate human behaviors in various\r\nscenarios such as education [6], human-machine interaction for\r\nsound design [57], health monitoring and intervention [34], etc.\r\nOur work shares similar spirit especially in the human-in-the-loop\r\nreinforcement learning. However, our work harnesses a dual-DRL\r\nframework to deal with the insufficient training data problem in\r\nthe human-in-the-loop training process, while others either use\r\na simplified algorithm [34, 57] to reduce the requirement of mass\r\ntraining data or leverage existing simulation environment [6]. The\r\nfuture work could focus on the exploration and comparison of these\r\ndifferent methodologies to tackle the problem of data insufficiency.\r\nIt is important to note that RL-based models may fail in certain\r\ncircumstances and there are still many challenges[68]. For instance,\r\nthe transfer from simulation to real environments is usually not\r\nperfect [29], which may deteriorate the performance of RL models.\r\nIn addition, a general model may not represent all users. For exam￾ple, in our user study results, there are still few participants whose\r\nresponse time is not improved by the regulation DRL agent. In\r\nthis case, a personalized model may potentially solve this problem.\r\nTherefore , we could fine-tune the pre-trained regulation DRL agent\r\nfor each real user to further improve the cognitive performance.\r\n8.2 Limitations of the User Study\r\nAlthough we have demonstrated the feasibility of our framework,\r\na few constraints need to be taken into account before studying its\r\nbroader implications.\r\n8.2.1 Study Population. Our user study recruited 80 participants\r\nto run a between-subjects evaluation. However, a larger-scale user\r\nstudy may be helpful to further verify the effectiveness of our\r\nframework. The reason is that the participants we recruited are\r\nmostly young people (age: around 20 years) with similar education\r\nbackground (undergraduates and graduates). A larger and more\r\ndiverse group of participants may reveal new insights and findings.\r\nIn addition, we may also explore the effectiveness of the regulation\r\nDRL agent in the long-term training of user cognition performance.\r\n8.2.2 Baseline Group Selection. In this work, we use Random\r\ngroup to compare with RL group. The reason has been clarified\r\nin Section 6.4. However, it is possible that there might be more\r\nadvanced feedback control strategies, e.g., Deep Tamer[69] and\r\nDeep COACH[3]. A comparison between these models and our\r\nframework may reveal new insights. We expect the open-source of\r\nour software/dataset can inspire the development of such models.\r\n8.2.3 Study Results and Findings. Our user study uses a two-way\r\nbetween-subjects ANOVA to demonstrate the significant differ￾ences between Random and RL group in response time change\r\nfrom Control session to Feedback session. However, we did not\r\nfind statistically significant differences in user emotion change\r\nincluding attention and anxiety change between the two groups,\r\nalthough differences exist in the attention/anxiety score distribu￾tion. One reason may be that participants just give subjective scores\r\nabout their feelings, which may vary significantly across different\r\nparticipants. Therefore, it is hard to normalize different participants’\r\nfeelings. A more standard emotion evaluation questionnaire may\r\nhave the potential to solve this problem such as the State-Trait\r\nAnxiety Inventory (STAI) questionnaire [62]. However, such eval￾uation consists of a large number of questions, which may stress\r\nthe participants and affect the results. A better way to explore user\r\nemotion change could be our future work.\r\n8.3 Generalization to Different Cognition Tasks\r\nand Feedback\r\nAs discussed in Sec. 1, the math arithmetic task and time pressure\r\nfeedback are used as example modalities to demonstrate the feasibil￾ity of our vision of augmenting human cognition using AI models.\r\nIn general, for other cognition tasks, we could similarly train a\r\ncorresponding simulation DRL agent to serve as the simulation\r\nenvironment. This is feasible since existing work has demonstrated\r\nthe power of neural network models in solving many cognition\r\ntasks[72]. Given the simulation DRL agent, we could similarly train\r\na regulation DRL agent to interact with the simulation environ￾ment. The trained regulation DRL agent can then be applied to\r\nregulate real users. Although we may need to collect new datasets\r\nfor new cognition tasks to train the simulation environment, it is\r\nalso possible to directly use the pre-trained regulation DRL agent\r\nin math arithmetic task to regulate users in other cognition tasks.\r\nThe reason is that the regulation DRL agent only takes user cogni￾tion results (response time) as observation space. It will not need\r\ncognition task information as input. The regulation DRL agent may\r\ncapture potential correlation between time pressure feedback con￾trol and user response time regardless of the task. We leave such\r\ngeneralization for future work.\r\nIn addition, our framework could also be extended to different\r\nfeedback modalities. Our current work uses visual time pressure\r\nfeedback to regulate user performance, which is also used in the\r\nobservation space of simulation DRL agent and action space of the\r\nregulation DRL agent. For other kinds of visual feedback, we could\r\nsimilarly use the corresponding visual information in the format\r\nof image/video. The format can be updated for other feedback\r\nmodalities. For example, for auditory feedback, we could use digital\r\naudio signals instead of image/video in the observation space of\r\nsimulation DRL agent and the action space of regulation DRL agent.\r\n9 CONCLUSION\r\nIn this paper, we present our exploration in intelligent feedback\r\nto augment human cognition performance by developing adaptive\r\ntime pressure visual feedback in a math arithmetic task. The control\nCognitive Exoskeleton: Augmenting Human Cognition with an AI-Mediated Intelligent Visual Feedback Conference ’23, 2023, Location\r\nstrategy is based on a regulation DRL agent, which is pre-trained\r\nby interacting with another simulation DRL agent. In a user study,\r\nwe demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of this framework\r\ndesign and provide explanation about how the regulation DRL\r\nagent could improve user cognition performance. We also discuss\r\npotential application scenarios, limitations, and future work to\r\nincrease the scalability of this work and make larger impact into\r\ndifferent research areas.
